# Design Heuristics & Behavioral Principles

This file holds two separate sets of evaluative principles used ONLY in the synthesis stage of a session, under a clearly separate "Heuristic Notes" section — never folded into how a persona reacts. Personas react based solely on their persona data (see personas-*.md); heuristics are a distinct analytical lens applied afterward, so you can see where data-grounded persona reactions and established design/behavioral principles agree or conflict.

Sources: Nielsen Norman Group's usability heuristics (nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics), and a set of consumer cognitive heuristics summarized from Emotiv's marketing blog (emotiv.com/blog/heuristic-examples). Content below is paraphrased in our own words for internal reference use, not reproduced verbatim.

---

## Part 1: Usability Heuristics (Nielsen Norman Group)

Ten broad rules of thumb for evaluating interface usability. Use these to check whether a design's *mechanics* support or undermine what a persona is trying to do — not to describe the persona's reaction itself.

1. **Visibility of system status** — The design should keep people informed about what's happening, with timely feedback after any action. Predictability builds trust.
2. **Match between the system and the real world** — Use the audience's own language and mental models, not internal jargon. Follow real-world conventions so the logic feels familiar rather than invented.
3. **User control and freedom** — People make mistakes; give them an easy, clearly marked way to back out of an action (undo, cancel, clear exit) without a lengthy process.
4. **Consistency and standards** — Don't make people guess whether different words or actions mean the same thing. Stay consistent internally, and follow conventions people already know from other products.
5. **Error prevention** — Better than a good error message is preventing the error in the first place — through good defaults, constraints, or a confirmation step before a risky action.
6. **Recognition rather than recall** — Minimize what someone has to remember. Keep necessary information (labels, options, context) visible or easily retrievable rather than requiring memory.
7. **Flexibility and efficiency of use** — Let both novice and expert users work efficiently — shortcuts and customization for experienced users shouldn't get in the way of first-timers.
8. **Aesthetic and minimalist design** — Every extra piece of content or visual noise competes with what actually matters. Keep the design focused on primary goals.
9. **Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors** — Error messages should be in plain language, state the problem precisely, and suggest a constructive next step.
10. **Help and documentation** — Ideally the design needs no explanation, but when it does, help content should be concise, task-focused, and available in context.

## Part 2: Consumer Cognitive Heuristics

Mental shortcuts that shape real-world purchasing decisions. Use these to explain *why* a persona might react the way their data-grounded profile suggests — or to flag when a design leans on one of these shortcuts in a way that could help or mislead.

1. **Availability heuristic** — People judge how common or good something is by how easily examples come to mind, not by an actual full accounting. Content formatted to be easy to recall (a few highlighted, scannable points) feels more persuasive than a large volume of unfiltered detail — regardless of whether it's more accurate.
2. **Brand awareness heuristic** — For lower-involvement purchases, familiarity itself often acts as a stand-in for quality, causing people to pick a known name without comparing alternatives. This can block a more thorough evaluation, especially for someone with no prior exposure to any of the options.
3. **Moral / values-based heuristic** — A single value-signaling term or label (e.g., an ethical or safety claim) can create a broad halo effect, leading people to assume the whole product or brand is more trustworthy than the specific claim actually supports. This impression tends to resist correction even when given more complete information later.
4. **Negativity bias** — Negative information carries disproportionately more weight in a decision than equally strong positive information. One credible negative point can outweigh several positive ones in someone's overall impression.
5. **Visual priming** — Images are processed faster than text and make positive information feel more concrete and actionable, but a picture doesn't fully counteract negative information already present in someone's mind.
6. **Primacy-recency effect** — People weight the first and last items in a sequence more heavily than what's presented in the middle, which tends to blur into the background. This matters for how content, reviews, or options are ordered.
7. **Alternative-based (whole-package) processing** — When someone already has a rough idea of what they want, they tend to compare entire options against each other as complete packages, rather than scanning individual attributes across many choices one at a time. Side-by-side comparison formats align with this natural processing style.

## How to apply these in a session synthesis

- Keep this section visually and structurally separate from the persona-grounded "Themes" section of your synthesis.
- Only cite a heuristic when it adds real explanatory value to something a persona actually reacted to — don't force-fit all 17 principles into every session.
- If a heuristic and a persona's data-grounded reaction point in different directions, say so explicitly rather than quietly picking one. That tension itself is often useful signal for the design team.
