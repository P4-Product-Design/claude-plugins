# Context playbooks

These set the bar for the audit. In Step 1, load:
1. The playbook for the **design type** (and traffic source, for landing pages).
2. **Trust factors**, every time.
3. **Older adults**, when the brand or audience skews 50+ (e.g. hearing, medical alert, AARP, NCOA).

If a context isn't covered here, research it (SKILL.md, Step 1).

---

## Landing page — paid social (Meta, TikTok)

The visitor was interrupted mid-scroll, so intent is low and patience is short. Most traffic is mobile.

- **Judge the mobile frame first.** The first screen must hook the visitor, show the offer, and show the CTA.
- **Match the ad:** same product, offer, words, and visual feel. Users who don't see what they clicked bounce.
- **One goal.** Strip the nav and outbound links. Promos must not compete with the primary CTA.
- **Proof early and near the CTA:** ratings plus creator or UGC-style content that matches the feed.
- **Short path.** Put a CTA within every screen or two. A sticky mobile CTA is common.
- **Answer objections inline,** close to the decision.
- **Swappable hero.** Creative burns out fast, so the hero should be easy to change per ad angle.
- **Lightweight media.** Slow heroes and autoplay video cost bounces on mobile data.

## Landing page — paid search and PMax (Google)

The visitor searched for something, so intent is higher. They want to confirm the page has it.

- **The hero shouldn't depend on one ad headline.** PMax writes the headlines and may send varied queries, so lead with core value and the product.
- **Product and price visible early.** Price, availability, and offer must match the Merchant Center feed and the ads.
- **Real page copy.** Google builds ad assets from page content.
- **One conversion goal.** Every section points to the primary action.
- **Proof and specifics.** Searchers compare, so give them concrete differentiators, ratings, and guarantees.

## Landing page — email

The visitor already knows the brand and clicked a specific promise.

- **Pick up where the email left off:** same offer, same visual. Don't re-introduce the brand.
- **Go straight to the offer or product.** Ask for less scrolling than a cold landing page would.

## Landing page — lead-gen and quiz (forms, click-to-call)

The visitor is trading personal information or time for an answer, a quote, or a call.

- **State the value of finishing above the form:** what they'll get, how fast, and that it's free.
- **Easy asks first, PII last.** Explain why each sensitive field is needed.
- **Show progress in steps** ("Step 2 of 4"). Let users go back without losing answers.
- **Click-to-call** visible on mobile, with hours, so users know someone will answer.
- **Privacy note next to the submit button,** not only in the footer.
- **Success state** that says what happens next and when.

## PDP (product detail page)

The visitor is deciding whether to buy this product. The first thing most users do is look at the images.

- **Mobile first screen:** image, name, rating, price, and the start of the buy box. Price and the add-to-cart path should start within the first screen.
- **Gallery.** Multiple angles, the supplement facts panel or label, a lifestyle shot, and zoom. Arrows and thumbnails at least 44px.
- **Rating next to the title.** It links to the reviews. The count and average must match the reviews section.
- **Buy box order:** variant → size or quantity → purchase type → add to cart. Keep long specs and benefit lists out of the path to the button.
- **Variants.** Use chips when there are 6 or fewer options. Preselect a default. Unavailable options say why ("Sold out", "Notify me"), not just gray.
- **Subscription.** Give it its own option card, preselected if subscribe is the goal. Show perks and flexibility ("skip, pause, cancel anytime") and both prices.
- **Risk reducers beside Add to Cart:** shipping threshold, returns or guarantee, and delivery estimate.
- **Add-to-cart feedback:** loading, then added (cart drawer or toast with a cart count), then error.
- **Sticky add to cart** once the buy box scrolls away on long PDPs. It carries the price and the selected variant.
- **Don't hide key content in subpages** on mobile (Baymard: users overlook it). Accordions are fine for secondary info.
- **Facts must agree.** Serving size, ingredients, flavors, and claims stay consistent from the buy box down to the FAQ.
- **Reviews:** a ratings breakdown, filters (by flavor or use), a verified-buyer badge, and photos.
- **Cross-sells** sit after the decision, never between the buyer and Add to Cart. Bundles need their own add button or a clear rule ("adds with your order").

## Product block (on-page commerce)

Baymard: most product lists underperform, and mobile does worse.

- **Card essentials:** image, name, price (with compare-at price if on sale), rating, and one key differentiator.
- **Clear hit areas.** The image and name lead somewhere predictable.
- **Variant choice that's easy:** a visible default. With few options, use swatches or chips instead of a hidden dropdown.
- **Add-to-cart feedback:** loading, added (with a cart cue or drawer), and error.
- **Same info order on every card.**
- **Quick view** for visually driven products.
- **States:** out of stock, sale, missing rating or image.

## Long-form content page (guides, education, SEO)

Visitors scan headings and skip body text (the layer-cake pattern), so they need to find their answer fast.

- **Answer first.** Put a summary or key takeaways above the fold.
- **Table of contents or jump links** on anything longer than about 4 screens. A sticky table of contents works on desktop.
- **Descriptive headings** that answer the question on their own.
- **Chunk the content:** short paragraphs, lists, tables, and callouts. Body text 16–18px, about 50–75 characters per line.
- **Byline, credentials, and an updated date** near the title.
- **Next step in context:** a product block or CTA at the natural decision points, not only at the end.
- **No dead end:** related content plus a CTA at the bottom.

## Affiliate review and best-of roundup

The visitor wants a trustworthy recommendation and a fast route to buy. Google's product-review guidance rewards evidence and comparison.

- **Top picks up top.** A summary table or cards with the winner per use case ("Best overall", "Best for side sleepers"), each with a CTA.
- **Evidence of first-hand testing:** original photos, test methods, measurements, and how many products were tested.
- **Pros and cons** for every product, plus who it's for and who should skip it.
- **Comparison table** with the factors buyers actually decide on.
- **Destination clarity.** CTAs name the retailer ("Check price at Amazon"). Show prices or ranges, and mark them as approximate when they change.
- **Disclosure next to the first affiliate link** and above the fold. A footer link to a disclosure page is not enough (FTC: "difficult to miss").
- **Methodology** link and an author with relevant credentials.
- **Consistent product blocks:** the same info order and CTA style for every pick.
- **Multiple sellers** where possible, so users can choose a retailer they trust.

## Comparison / "X vs Y" page

The visitor is down to two or three options and wants a verdict.

- **Verdict first:** who should pick each option, in one line apiece.
- **Side-by-side table** with matching rows. Highlight the deciding differences.
- **Named competitors** with fair, sourced claims. A vague "Competitor" reads as made up.
- **A CTA for each option.** On your own brand's page, give a clear primary CTA.

## Collection / category page

The visitor is browsing a set of products and narrowing down. Baymard rates most product lists mediocre or worse, and mobile does worse.

- **Category intro is short.** One or two lines plus a way to narrow, not a hero that pushes products below the first screen.
- **Filters by what buyers actually decide on** (goal, flavor, price, dietary needs). On mobile, put filters behind one visible button with an active-filter count.
- **Sort options** include best sellers, price, and rating.
- **Product cards** follow the product block playbook: the same info order, rating, price, and a clear hit area.
- **Results count** and applied-filter chips that are easy to clear.
- **No-results state** with a reset and suggestions.
- **Users keep their place** when they come back from a PDP (no jump to the top).
- **Guided selling** ("Not sure? Take the quiz", "Build your stack") for shoppers who don't know what they need.

## Homepage

Visitors arrive with different goals. The page routes them; it rarely converts them.

- **Say who you are and what you sell** in the first screen, in plain words.
- **Clear paths for each main intent:** shop best sellers, browse categories, learn, find a deal. Make 3–5 obvious entry points, not 10.
- **One featured offer at a time.** Rotating carousels split attention, and most users never see slide 2.
- **Proof early:** ratings, review counts, press, and trust badges near the top.
- **Best sellers or top picks** as a product block, so ready buyers can act without navigating.
- **Returning visitors:** easy routes to the account, reorders, and subscriptions.
- **Footer as a backup nav** for users who scroll to the end.

## Quiz results page

The visitor just invested time answering questions. This page decides whether that effort pays off.

- **Show the recommendation immediately:** the product or plan, and why it fits *their* answers ("Because you said…").
- **One clear primary pick**, with one or two alternatives at most.
- **Price and CTA next to the recommendation,** not after more content.
- **Let users edit their answers** without starting over.
- **Proof for the pick:** reviews from people like them.
- **Save or email the results** for users who aren't ready yet.
- **No gate after the effort.** If the result is behind an email or phone field, say so before the quiz starts.

## Partner co-branded page (e.g. AARP, NCOA)

The partner's name is the trust signal. The page has to use it without confusing who sells what.

- **Make the relationship clear above the fold:** whose offer this is, what the partner's role is, and the member benefit.
- **Lead with the partner's brand** if the audience came from the partner, and keep the product brand clearly secondary or co-equal.
- **Show the member benefit or pricing specifically,** and how to claim it (member number, code, call).
- **Follow the partner's brand and compliance rules:** approved logos, required disclaimers, and no implied endorsement beyond the agreement.
- **Click-to-call and hours** for audiences that prefer the phone.
- **Load the Older adults modifier** when the partner's audience is 50+.

## Trust factors (always load)

Users decide in seconds whether to believe a page. Check that each of these is present, specific, and placed where the decision happens.

- **Social proof at the ask:** rating, review count, and a recent or relevant review near the CTA. All numbers match across the page.
- **Specific claims, sourced:** numbers, test results, and named labs or experts. Superlatives need proof, and every asterisk needs its footnote.
- **Risk reversal near the CTA:** guarantee, returns, free shipping threshold, cancel anytime.
- **Who's behind it:** a real author or expert, credentials, an updated date, and contact options.
- **Third-party validation:** certifications, lab tests, press, and awards. Make them live text or links, not baked-in images.
- **Disclosure:** affiliate, sponsored, and FDA or medical disclaimers present and easy to find, not hidden.
- **Consistency:** price, rating, offer, and product facts identical everywhere they appear. A mismatch costs more trust than a missing element.
- **Polish near money:** no layout bugs, overflow, or typos around price, the CTA, or checkout cues (aesthetic-usability effect).
- **No pressure tactics:** no fake timers, pre-checked boxes, or guilt-trip declines.

## Older adults (50+) — audience modifier

Apply on top of the design-type playbook. NN/g found websites about twice as hard for seniors to use as for younger users.

- **Larger text:** body 18px or more where possible, never under 16px. High contrast, no light gray body copy.
- **Bigger targets:** 44px minimum, generous spacing, no small text links for key actions.
- **Plain labels:** say what happens ("Call a specialist", not "Get started"). No icon-only controls.
- **Phone number visible** and tappable, with hours. Many users prefer to call.
- **No hidden interactions:** avoid hover-only menus, swipe-only carousels, and auto-advancing content.
- **One task per screen** in forms. Show progress, let users go back, and show errors in plain words next to the field.
- **Trust matters more:** a recognizable brand or partner (e.g. AARP), real people, and clear pricing with no surprises.
